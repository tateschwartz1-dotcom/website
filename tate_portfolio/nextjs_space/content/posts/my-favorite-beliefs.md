# You don’t know better than the data

One summer, for my annual giant project, I started a t-shirt business.

Inspired by [City Connect jerseys](https://www.espn.com/mlb/story/_/id/39776958/tracking-mlb-city-connect-jerseys-uniforms), I settled on selling designs connected to my hometown. You can check out the five shirts [here](/projects).

(20% of small businesses [fail](https://www.commerceinstitute.com/business-failure-rate/) in the first year. I knew it, but not for me.)

When ordering plain T-shirts to press designs on, I had to guess the demand for each size. The [internet](https://undergroundshirts.com/blog/t-shirt-sizing-the-ultimate-guide) had this advice:

[IMAGE: beliefs-tshirt-sizes.png]

It didn’t feel right. A third should be XL or bigger? I’m larger than most others, and I can fit into a medium, and I’m probably going to sell to people similar to my age, and I…

I changed the ratio for my order and ended up needing more XL+ sizes. The business was also a failure.

Before, I simply knew that data knows better. Now, it shakes my bones. It yells at me from the back of my mind. I needed the loss to turn theory I trusted into real change in my behavior.

Another tale: In 1954, Paul Meehl reviewed about 20 studies pitting simple formulas against expert judgment predicting grades, parole violations and response to electroshock therapy. He found that in nearly every case, the simple formulas did as well as or better than humans.

[IMAGE: beliefs-meehl.jpg | small]

Researchers were upset with Meehl’s findings, so the experiment became one of the most replicated in all of social science. They arrived at the same result.

Robyn Dawes took it a step further, [discovering](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf) that giving equal weights to everything in the equation still was reliably better than expert judgment.

[IMAGE: beliefs-dawes.jpg | small]

[IMAGE: beliefs-dawes-math.png]

Even [when](https://meehl.umn.edu/sites/meehl.umn.edu/files/files/138cstixdawesfaustmeehl.pdf) experts were given the formula’s answer and allowed to override it, they still did worse than the formula alone. They kept spotting “exceptions” that weren’t really exceptions.

One of the reasons for the madness is the perfect consistency of formulas and the terrible consistency of us. Even if our judgment is completely unbiased, we don’t apply it the same repeatedly, causing more error than bad weighting.

[This book](https://replicationindex.com/2020/12/30/a-meta-scientific-perspective-on-thinking-fast-and-slow/) and [this game](https://www.quantifiedintuitions.org/estimation-game) made me experience how untrustworthy particular types of human decision making are. We can build intuition after experiencing a set of instant results. We are horrible under uncertainty with delayed feedback.

# Better to be lucky than good

This was Drew Campbell’s catchphrase during the competition show Big Brother. He had saved himself repeatedly with lucky challenge wins at desperate times. At the finale, he had the highest odds to take home the grand prize. Instead, all of the worst things happened, and he went home empty-handed as a tragic hero.

The best does not win every time. This includes the 2016 Warriors, Citizen Kane and the Beatles.

There do not have to be dice or cards for uncertainty. The weather, the national news, the exact spin of the ball, health, the order and selection of questions and challenges; these all are things that we do not fully control but impact every outcome.

As much as we would like the hero who has trained the hardest and become the best to win, there is no getting rid of uncertainty (i.e. chance / variation / luck / big moments / error).

There is so much variance that you can’t simply trust the numbers. The [Optimizer’s Curse](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/q2TfTirvspCTH2vbZ/the-best-cause-will-disappoint-you-an-intro-to-the) makes it difficult to use estimations.

[IMAGE: beliefs-optimizers-curse.webp]

When making estimates and picking to invest in the best, you are biased toward picking high-variance options that get lucky with the uncertainty during the estimation. Even if the estimates are unbiased and are just off due to inherent variance, picking the top guess will lead you, on average, to be disappointed with a worse true performance.

Variance applies to estimations and the results themselves. If success is made up of talent and luck, how do you know if doing well isn’t just a coincidence?

You don’t; instead, you know that it’s likely for the next result to be worse. A good result means you likely had good luck, so future normal luck will be worse in comparison, leading to a probable worse result. This regression to the mean with variance means it’s hard to say anything you did different “worked.”

I’m not too hard on myself. If I don’t have full control, why should I feel full pride or guilt? You win some, you lose some. Stay humble, stay confident. The best way to combat the variance is with sample size. Trying again, over and over.

I’m already incredibly lucky. I’m financially stable in a rich country and have two parents. If I were born again as a random person on the planet, it is extremely likely my life would be worse. My day-to-day luck looks like a rounding error in comparison.

# If you’re not embarrassed, you’re not trying hard enough

I have made a fool of myself this past year.

I joined my college’s startup community. They spoke a different language. SaaS, model context protocols, MVPs. My only weapons were quick Googling, dumb questions and faking until making.

I started writing and producing music. I’ve had to give up any hope of replicating what it sounds like in my head. I don’t think recording in a nice studio instead of under a thick blanket when my roommate is away will make the vocals sound better.

I added skirts to my wardrobe. Excruciatingly painful.

I decided to write all of this out for anyone to see. It’s embarrassing, but so is everything else on this list. I do it all despite the shame and failure because I want the growth. I’m becoming a better person, at a steep cost I wince at paying.

To cope, I reason it’s more embarrassing to not try so much that you publicly fail. What are you, scared? Satisfied with an unremarkable life? For me, waking up and realizing I didn’t do what I truly wanted is a worse nightmare.

# Nothing is a resource

I think people undervalue space. Having nothing. A lack. A clean slate.

The default of keeping is intuitive. “It’s better than nothing, no?”

Maybe. What about the opportunity cost? The loss of flexibility? The ugliness of clutter?

Nothing is properly valued in professional sports. The salary cap means you can only pay your team so much. A bad player isn’t just unusable, but they also represent money that could have purchased better ones.

Players aren’t judged on how many points they score, but whether they justified their salary. Commentators and fans easily recognize that players can be good, but not good enough for the resources invested in them.

Teams love nothing. Not paying the maximum means flexibility. If a surprise signing opportunity happens, they can capitalize. If someone gets hurt, they have the ability to pivot and compensate.

So many of the opportunities, people and interests that define my life thrive because I had space available for them. This was only possible by cutting without knowing what would fill the void, or when.

It is uncomfortable to have no backup plan or replacements. It is also uncomfortable to be tied down to bad deals that eat too much of your time and energy.

Time is the most valuable resource I have. After making a mistake, I remind myself that people would pay millions to reset to their 20s, be poor and have a fresh career ahead of them. The nothingness of an open future is too valuable to waste.

[One reason](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2937956?seq=1) for this waste is that we are aversive to loss. To overcome the bias, [it does work](https://isdm.nyu.edu/2019/06/22/thinking-like-a-trader/) to “think like a trader” or a sports team manager.

I’m off to go win some championships.
