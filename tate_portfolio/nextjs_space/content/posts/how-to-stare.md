---
title: How to stare at something for 10 minutes
date: 2026-11-13
---

I stumbled across [this article](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/11/09/insider/10-minute-challenge.html) about setting a 10-minute timer and staring at a single painting for all of it.

For months now, I have been staring. Here are some of the health benefits:

- Would you like to never be bored again? Reps of staring train you to never be bored again.
- Like a nice hot shower, it’s something to look forward to every ~~month~~ day.
- You gain an intimate appreciation of the art. The piece is now familiar, easy to take in and feels like a stranger-turned-friendly associate who remembers your name.
- Once you master staring at paintings, you notice photos are like really detailed paintings. Isn’t real life a really detailed photo? Now, you always have art to enjoy at your disposal.

Unfortunately, the New York Times throws you into painting stimulation with little guidance, only offering a discussion question or two. This guide hopes to better prepare you for the journey that is a stare session.

# Prepare

You will need:

- **A timer:** You are not allowed to look at the timer during your stare. During your first session, you will be distracted with paranoia over if the timer will work or not. It is essential to test it beforehand to have full confidence. If you do not wish to cause a nuisance, vibration-only timers are available to download.
- **Interests:** Before you dive in, assemble your toolkit. What are some of your interests? Things you like to daydream about? Imagine? Solidify a list of three. They’re a surprise tool that will help us later.
- **A light:** In settings where it is appropriate to get up close and personal with the art, a flashlight improves the experience immensely. Not only does it reveal details and texture, but buying into the fantasy of being a detective/curator/adventurer inspecting important artifacts is thrilling.

# The first minutes

Are overwhelming. Your eyes will dart. The lack of direction will be uncomfortable.

Your goal for the first few minutes is to take everything in. Jump into the water and get used to it. Let your focus jump. Don’t fight it.

Notice the subjects. The background. The frame. The setting. The presentation. The look in relation to what is nearby.

Once you’re over the initial shock, try to systematically notice everything. Whether you go row by row or part by part, turn over all the stones. You don’t have to spend more than a few seconds on everything, just leave no room for doubt that you missed important detail.

# Then on

Once you’ve settled in, now you can pick something to focus on.

You will be bored. There’s discomfort in not being interested, and agony in having little hope that you will be.

This is where pain and gain happen. Eventually, your mind will find something it attaches itself to ponder. Afterward, you will feel proud and accomplished. When all hope was lost, you were able to make interest out of nothing. A heroic act.

Here are strategies for this:

- Translate your interests. Infuse what you like into the art.
  - I like sports, shopping and movies.
  - Imagine the world your art represents or depicts. What would a sports area look like there? A store? What movie takes place?
- Visualize different perspectives, such as a different angle, point of view or audience type.
- How would you sell the art to someone? This is a great way to bias yourself into liking it.
- Imagine the story of how the art (or what it depicts) was made.

Sometimes, the freedom to think anything can be overwhelming. Art helps by supplying a sense of direction.

For example, I was staring at this post card.

While inspecting the text on the bottom, it reminded me of how I sign my name. Is there a cooler way to sign my name? The complete freedom of how I can make “Tate Schwartz” look was intimidating. Answering the question “how would I sign my name in this style?” was a much easier question to answer. I then went on to think about menu design from there.

You’re now as prepared as you’ll ever be. Go stare at something in your room, even if you’ve glanced at it hundreds of times. A stare is worth a million looks.
