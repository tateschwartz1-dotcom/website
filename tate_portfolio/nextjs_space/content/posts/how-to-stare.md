I stumbled across [this article](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/11/09/insider/10-minute-challenge.html) about staring at a single painting for the entirety of a 10-minute timer.

For months now, I have been staring. Here are some of the health benefits:

- Would you like to never be bored again? Reps of this train yourself to find entertainment in anything.
- Like a nice hot shower, it’s a habit to look forward to every day.
- You gain an intimate appreciation of the art. The piece is now familiar, easy to take in and feels like a stranger-turned-friendly associate who remembers your name.
- Once you master staring at paintings, you notice photos are really detailed paintings. And isn’t real life a really detailed photo? You now always have art to enjoy at your disposal.

Unfortunately, the New York Times throws you into this activity with little guidance, only offering a discussion question or two. This guide will better prepare you for a stare session.

# Prepare

You will need:

- **A timer:** You are not allowed to look at the timer during your stare. Because of this, you will be distracted with paranoia over if the timer will work or not. It is essential to test it beforehand to have full confidence. If you do not wish to cause a nuisance, vibration-only timers are available.
- **Interests:** Before you dive in, assemble your toolkit. What are some of your interests? Things you like to daydream about? Imagine? Solidify a list of three. They’re a surprise tool that will help us later.
- **A light:** In settings where it is appropriate to get up close and personal with the art, a flashlight immensely improves the experience. Not only does it reveal details and texture, but buying into the fantasy of being a detective/curator/adventurer inspecting important artifacts is fun.

# The first minutes

Are overwhelming. Your eyes will dart. The lack of direction will be uncomfortable.

Your goal for the first few minutes is to take everything in. Jump into the water and get used to it.

Notice the background. The frame. The presentation. The look in relation to what is nearby.

Once you’re over the initial shock, try to systematically acknowledge everything. Whether you go row by row or part by part, turn over all the stones. You don’t have to spend more than a few seconds on every part, just leave no room for doubt that you missed important detail.

# Then on

Once you’ve settled in, now you can pick something to focus on.

You will be bored. There is discomfort in not being interested, and agony in having little hope that you will be.

This is where pain and gain happen. Eventually, no matter how long it takes, your mind will naturally focus on something. Afterward, you will feel proud and accomplished. When all hope was lost, you were able to make interest out of nothing. A heroic act.

Here are strategies for this:

- Translate your interests. Infuse what you like into the art.
  - I like sports, shopping and movies.
  - Imagine the world your art represents or depicts. What would a sports arena look like there? A themed store? What movie takes place?
- Picture different perspectives, such as a different angle, point of view or audience type.
- How would you sell the art to someone? This biases yourself into liking it.
- Tell the story of how the art (or what it depicts) was made.

Sometimes, the freedom to think about anything is overwhelming. Art can help by supplying a sense of direction.

For example, I was staring at this post card.

[IMAGE: stare-postcard.jpg]

While inspecting the text on the bottom, I thought about my signature. Is there a cooler way to sign my name? The complete freedom of creative potential was intimidating. Answering the question “how would I sign my name in this style?” was much less daunting and much more enjoyable. I then went on to think about menu design with the font, then what a restaurant would look like on this monochrome island, then imagining it full and celebratory with peculiar locals after a local soccer match.

You’re now as prepared as you’ll ever be. Go stare at something in your room, even if you’ve glanced at it hundreds of times. A stare is worth a million looks.
